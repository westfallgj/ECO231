### **Chapter 6 - The Macroeconomic Perspective**

  * **Self Check Question \#1: What is the difference between a recession and a depression?**
    A recession is a significant, widespread, and prolonged downturn in economic activity. A common rule of thumb is that a recession occurs when the real GDP (Gross Domestic Product) decreases for two consecutive quarters (six months). A depression is a much more severe and long-lasting recession. While there's no official definition for a depression, it's characterized by a deep and sustained decline in output and a very high unemployment rate, like the Great Depression of the 1930s.

  * **Self Check Question \#2: Why is it unfair to compare the GDP of the United States with the GDP of Jordan?**
    It is unfair to directly compare the total GDP of the United States and Jordan because the two countries have vastly different population sizes. The U.S. has a much larger population, so its total GDP will naturally be larger. A more meaningful comparison is **GDP per capita**, which is calculated by dividing a country's total GDP by its population. This gives an average measure of economic output per person, providing a better sense of the economic well-being of the average citizen in each country.

  * **Review Question \#11: What is GDP per capita?**
    GDP per capita is a measure of a country's economic output per person. It is calculated by dividing the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of a country by its total population.

  * **Review Question \#12: Which is the better measure for comparing the standard of living between countries—GDP or GDP per capita? Why?**
    **GDP per capita** is the better measure for comparing the standard of living. Total GDP can be misleading because a country might have a large GDP simply because it has a large population (like China or India). GDP per capita provides an average of the economic output available to each person, which gives a more accurate picture of the economic well-being and standard of living for the average individual in that country.

  * **Review Question \#14: What is the difference between nominal GDP and real GDP?**

      * **Nominal GDP** is the value of all goods and services produced in an economy measured at *current* market prices. It does not account for the effects of inflation.
      * **Real GDP** is the value of all goods and services produced in an economy measured at *constant* prices from a specific base year. Real GDP is adjusted for inflation and is a more accurate measure of actual economic growth.

  * **Review Question \#15: What is the difference between the GDP deflator and the Consumer Price Index?**

      * The **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** measures the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a *fixed market basket* of consumer goods and services. It reflects the prices of things people buy.

  * **Review Question \#19: What does a GDP of a country not capture?**
    GDP does not capture several important aspects of well-being, including:

      * **Non-market transactions:** GDP doesn't include unpaid work like household chores, childcare, or volunteer services.
      * **Leisure time:** A higher GDP might come at the cost of less leisure time, which affects the quality of life.

      * **Environmental quality:** GDP doesn't account for negative externalities like pollution that can harm the standard of living.
      * **The level of inequality:** GDP per capita is an average and doesn't show how income is distributed among the population.

  * **Critical Thinking Question \#21: The U.S. has a GDP of approximately $23 trillion. Does this mean every person in the U.S. has about $70,000 worth of goods and services?**
    No, it does not. The $70,000 figure is the GDP per capita, which is a statistical average (Total GDP / population). It does not reflect the actual distribution of income. In reality, income and wealth are distributed unevenly, with some individuals having significantly more than $70,000 worth of goods and services and many having significantly less.

  * **Critical Thinking Question \#23: Why do you think that the underground economy is larger in developing countries?**
    The underground economy is often larger in developing countries for several reasons:

      * **High Taxes and Regulation:** When taxes are high and government regulations are burdensome, businesses and individuals have a stronger incentive to operate "off the books" to avoid these costs.
      * **Lack of Formal Institutions:** Developing countries may have less developed financial and legal systems, making it difficult for small businesses to operate formally.
      * **High Unemployment:** A lack of formal job opportunities can push people into informal, unreported work as a means of survival.

### **Chapter 11 - Aggregate Demand/Aggregate Supply**

  * **Self Check Questions \#1-6, 8 & 9, 11-13:**

    1.  The **aggregate supply (AS) curve** shows the total quantity of output (real GDP) that firms will produce and sell at each price level.
    2.  The **aggregate demand (AD) curve** shows the total spending on domestic goods and services at each price level.
    3.  **Keynes' Law** ("demand creates its own supply") is most relevant in the **short run**, especially during a recession when there are many unemployed resources.
    4.  **Say's Law** ("supply creates its own demand") is most relevant in the **long run**, as the economy's potential to supply goods and services (its potential GDP) is the ultimate determinant of its size.
    5.  The **horizontal axis** of the AD/AS diagram represents **Real GDP**, and the **vertical axis** represents the **Price Level** (like the CPI or GDP deflator).
    6.  The **upward slope of the AS curve** in the short run is due to "sticky" input prices. When the price level for final goods rises, but the price of inputs (like wages) does not immediately rise, firms have a profit incentive to increase production.
    7.  The three reasons the AD curve slopes downward are:
          * **The Wealth Effect:** A lower price level increases the real value of wealth, encouraging more consumption.
          * **The Interest Rate Effect:** A lower price level means people need less money to make purchases, which tends to lower interest rates and encourage investment spending.
          * **The Foreign Price Effect:** A lower price level in the U.S. makes U.S. goods cheaper for foreign buyers and foreign goods more expensive for U.S. buyers, increasing net exports.
    8.  A component of aggregate demand is **C + I + G + (X - M)**, which stands for Consumption, Investment, Government spending, and Net Exports (Exports minus Imports).
    9.  A higher price for a key input (like oil) would cause the **short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve to shift to the left**, meaning less real GDP would be supplied at every price level.
    10. An increase in consumer confidence would cause the **aggregate demand (AD) curve to shift to the right**, as consumers would be willing to spend more at every price level.
    11. If the AD curve shifts to the right while the economy is in the steep (neoclassical) part of the AS curve, the result will be a higher **price level (inflation)** with little or no increase in real GDP.

  * **Review Questions \#16-36:** These are all good, but let's focus on a few key concepts.

      * **Shifts in AD:** Any change in C, I, G, or (X-M) that is not caused by a change in the price level will shift the AD curve. For example, a tax cut (increases C), a rise in business confidence (increases I), an increase in government spending (increases G), or a boom in a trading partner's economy (increases X) will all shift AD to the right.
      * **Shifts in AS:** Changes in productivity or the price of key inputs will shift the AS curve. For example, a technological advance (increases productivity) shifts AS to the right. An increase in the price of oil shifts AS to the left.
      * **Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS):** In the long run, the AS curve is vertical at the level of **potential GDP**. At this point, all resources are fully employed, and changes in aggregate demand only affect the price level, not output.
      * **Stagflation:** This occurs when the SRAS curve shifts to the left. The result is a combination of a stagnant economy (higher unemployment, lower GDP) and inflation (a higher price level).

### **Chapter 12 - The Keynesian Perspective**

  * **Self Check Questions \#1-7:**

    1.  The Keynesian perspective focuses on the idea that **aggregate demand** is the primary driver of the economy, especially in the short run. It argues that due to sticky wages and prices, the economy can get stuck in a recessionary period with high unemployment if total demand is too low.
    2.  A **recessionary gap** is the situation where the equilibrium level of real GDP is *less than* the potential GDP level. An **inflationary gap** is when the equilibrium level of real GDP is *greater than* potential GDP.
    3.  The key building blocks of Keynesian analysis are that aggregate demand is not always sufficient to create full employment and that wages and prices can be "sticky" downwards.
    4.  Sticky wages and prices are wages and prices that do not adjust quickly to changes in economic conditions. For example, workers are often unwilling to accept nominal wage cuts, so wages are sticky downwards.
    5.  The **expenditure multiplier** is the idea that an initial change in spending (e.g., government spending) can lead to a larger final change in real GDP. For example, if the government spends $100, that becomes income for someone, who then spends a portion of it, which becomes income for someone else, and so on.
    6.  The Phillips Curve shows the short-run tradeoff between inflation and unemployment.
    7.  A key reason for the Phillips Curve tradeoff is that a rise in aggregate demand increases GDP, which lowers unemployment but also puts upward pressure on prices, causing inflation.

  * **Review Questions \#8-16 (omit 14):**

      * The main disagreement between Keynesian and Neoclassical economists is over how quickly wages and prices adjust. Keynesians believe they are sticky, especially downwards, meaning the economy can get stuck below full employment for long periods. Neoclassicals believe wages and prices are flexible and that the economy will return to its potential GDP on its own relatively quickly.
      * According to Keynes, the solution to a recession is to use **expansionary fiscal policy** (cutting taxes or increasing government spending) to shift the AD curve to the right, closing the recessionary gap.
      * The solution to inflation is to use **contractionary fiscal policy** (raising taxes or cutting government spending) to shift the AD curve to the left, closing the inflationary gap.

  * **Critical Thinking Questions \#20 & 23:**

      * **\#20:** The impact of the expenditure multiplier is reduced in an open economy because some of the increased income will be spent on **imports**. This spending leaks out of the domestic economy and does not generate further income for domestic producers, thus weakening the multiplier effect compared to a closed economy.
      * **\#23:** Keynesian economics is sometimes referred to as "demand-side" economics because its central focus is on managing aggregate demand (AD) to achieve macroeconomic goals. The core Keynesian argument is that the level of economic activity is determined by the level of total demand, and that government intervention may be necessary to ensure demand is sufficient for full employment.

### **Chapter 13 - The Neoclassical Perspective**

  * **Self Check Questions \#3 & 4:**
    3\.  The two building blocks of neoclassical analysis are:
    \* **Potential GDP determines the size of the economy:** In the long run, the economy's output is limited by its productive capacity (potential GDP).
    \* **Wages and prices are flexible:** Neoclassicals believe that prices and wages will adjust to bring the economy back to its potential GDP level.
    4\.  **Rational expectations** is the theory that people form the most accurate possible expectations about the future that they can, using all information available to them. In the neoclassical model, this means people will anticipate the inflationary effects of expansionary policy, rendering it ineffective at changing real GDP even in the short run.

  * **Review Questions \#5-16 (omit 15):**

      * Neoclassical economists believe the economy will self-correct from a recession. If output is below potential, high unemployment will eventually lead to falling wages. Lower wages reduce costs for firms, shifting the **SRAS curve to the right** until the economy returns to potential GDP at a lower price level.
      * The neoclassical view of the Phillips Curve is that it is **vertical in the long run** at the **natural rate of unemployment**. They argue that there is no permanent tradeoff between inflation and unemployment. Any attempt to use expansionary policy to hold unemployment below its natural rate will only result in accelerating inflation.
      * The primary policy focus for neoclassical economists is on long-run growth. They advocate for policies that shift the long-run aggregate supply (potential GDP) to the right. This includes policies that encourage investment in human capital (education), physical capital (machinery), and technology.

  * **Critical Thinking Questions \#18 & 19:**

      * **\#18:** A neoclassical economist would likely argue that the government should do nothing or take a hands-off approach to a mild recession. They would believe that wages and prices will adjust flexibly, and the economy's self-correcting mechanisms will return it to potential GDP without the need for intervention, which could be destabilizing.
      * **\#19:** If the government enacts an expansionary fiscal policy (e.g., a tax cut) but the public believes it is only temporary, the effect on aggregate demand will likely be weak. People, having rational expectations, will understand that their after-tax income will fall again in the future when the cut is reversed. Therefore, they are likely to **save** most of the temporary increase in income rather than spend it, leading to a very small shift in the AD curve.
